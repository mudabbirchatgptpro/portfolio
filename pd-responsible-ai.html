Responsible AI for Your Classroom

A 90-minute working session for K-12 teachers · 15 to 30 participants · Laptops required, no prior AI experience assumed
This is a demonstration session plan drawn from professional development I have designed and delivered to school staff on evidence-based instruction and responsible AI use. Names, examples, and materials are generalized; the structure and facilitation moves are the ones I actually use.

The problem this session solves

Most AI PD fails in one of two ways. It either evangelizes tools without judgment, so teachers walk out with ten apps and no principles, or it moralizes about risk without practice, so teachers walk out anxious and change nothing. This session is built so every teacher leaves having done three things with their own real material: produced something useful with an AI tool, caught the tool making an error, and written one classroom norm they will actually enforce.

Outcomes

By the end of 90 minutes, each participant has:

  • Adapted one of their own upcoming lessons or materials using an AI tool, and can say what they kept, changed, and rejected.
  • Found and documented at least one confident error the tool made in their own subject area.
  • Drafted one student-facing norm for AI use in their classroom, tied to that error.
  • A one-page decision guide: where AI drafts, where the teacher decides.

Design principles

Adults learn on their own material
Every activity uses the participant's real lesson, real rubric, real parent email. Nothing generic to "try later."
Skepticism is taught by experience
The error hunt is scheduled, not left to chance. Teachers trust their own catch more than any warning slide.
Judgment over tools
The deliverable is a decision guide and a norm, not a tool list. Tools change; the boundary between drafting and deciding does not.
Follow-through is designed in
The session ends with a two-week commitment and a named partner, because PD without a next touch decays fast.

The 90 minutes

MinutesSegmentWhat happensFacilitator moves
0 to 10Cold open: two artifactsParticipants compare two lesson hooks, one teacher-written, one AI-drafted, and vote which is which. Reveal: most rooms split near 50/50.Do not resolve the discomfort. Name the real question: not "can it write?" but "who decides if it is good?"
10 to 25Make something realEach teacher takes one lesson they will teach in the next two weeks and uses an AI tool to differentiate it for two learner profiles they actually have.Circulate. Push past first drafts: "ask it for three versions and reject two."
25 to 40The error huntPairs swap subjects and try to make the tool produce a confident error in the partner's discipline, then log the best catch on a shared wall.Celebrate catches loudly. The energy of this segment carries the session's core lesson.
40 to 55Sort the wallWhole group sorts the error wall: harmless, misleading, harmful-if-graded. Each category gets a different classroom response.Press one question on every card: "what would a student have needed to know to catch this?"
55 to 75Draft the boundaryEach teacher fills a one-page decision guide: tasks where AI drafts first, tasks where it never touches, and the checks in between. Then writes one student-facing norm.Share two strong examples mid-drafting, not at the end, so revision happens in the room.
75 to 90Commit and pairEach teacher names the one lesson they will run with their adapted material, picks an accountability partner, and books a ten-minute debrief for two weeks out.Collect the guides; photograph the norm wall. These become the follow-up session's opening data.

Differentiation for the adults in the room

The skeptic
Gets the error hunt first in spirit: their doubt becomes the room's quality control, named and valued out loud.
The enthusiast
Gets the "reject two of three drafts" constraint, which converts speed into judgment.
The overwhelmed
Works the whole session on a single lesson they already had to prepare, so the PD subtracts from their week instead of adding to it.
The leader in the room
Gets the sorted error wall and the collected guides as ready-made inputs for a school-level AI policy conversation.

Why this shape

The session runs on the same loop I design into student curriculum: make something, test it against reality, and let the failure teach the principle. Teachers do not adopt norms because a slide told them to. They adopt norms they wrote themselves, in response to an error they personally caught, on material they personally need. Ninety minutes is enough for that loop to complete once, and once is enough to change the default.
